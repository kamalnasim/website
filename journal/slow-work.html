Making music independently.

A personal reflection on the freedom, discipline, and uncertainty of building a creative life on your own terms.

A journal entry

There is a particular kind of freedom in making something because it matters to you. Not because it is expected, not because it fits a trend, and not because it is designed to please anyone else. It is a different kind of energy altogether. You learn, you experiment, you make mistakes, and you keep going anyway.

I have come to think of independent music as a form of trust. Trust in your own ear, in your own instincts, and in the rough work that often comes before something feels complete. A song may begin as a fragment, a melody in a voice note, a line that sounds unfinished in the moment, or an idea you are not even sure belongs to you yet. But once it starts growing, the real work begins.

The process is rarely clean. A release may take three minutes to listen to and three months, or three years, to become. There are revisions, false starts, recordings that do not work, and the slow work of deciding what is honest enough to keep. There are moments when you think something is finished, only to hear it again and realise it is still searching for its own shape. That is part of it. The song is not just the idea; it is the process of learning what it needs.

I have learned that independence is not only about doing everything yourself. It is also about being willing to sit with uncertainty. It is about choosing what to release when it is still imperfect, and what to leave behind when it no longer belongs to the version of you who made it. It is a strange and deeply personal kind of responsibility.

There is something liberating about not waiting for permission. You can keep writing without needing a gatekeeper to validate the work. You can follow the sound that feels true, even if it is not the one that fits neatly into a category or a market. You can make work that is not polished for anyone else’s taste, and in doing so, you discover what actually matters to you. That is not always easy, but it is often the most honest route.

At the same time, independence asks for patience. It asks for humility. It asks you to become a listener to your own work, not just a creator of it. The songs do not always arrive ready. Sometimes they take time to reveal themselves. Sometimes the revision is not about improving the song but understanding why you wanted it in the first place.

I think that is why I still return to this way of making music. It keeps me close to the work. It reminds me that the value of a song is not only in how it sounds at the end, but in the decisions, the discipline, and the honesty that lead to it. The finished piece is never only the result. It is evidence of the process, the effort, and the version of the artist who was willing to keep making it.

Making music independently is not a shortcut, and it is not a fantasy of total freedom. It is a practice of living with your own standards, learning from your own mistakes, and staying open enough to keep growing. That is the part I keep returning to: not the illusion of control, but the clarity that comes from making something real in your own voice.
